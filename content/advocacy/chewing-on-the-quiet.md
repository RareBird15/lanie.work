I bought a chew tool. It's a small piece of silicone I can bite down on, and it's in my mouth a lot now.

Before I had it, I tried not to chew on my fingers. That meant not stimming when I needed to. I'd catch myself doing it and stop, and then I'd just sit there with the need still running under my skin.

Now I chew freely. It's better.

But the more I use it, the more I understand what the need actually is.

## What I'm Not Getting

I live in a nursing home. I'm thirty-four. Most of the people here are elderly, and the kitchen can make regular solid food. It just doesn't for me. My GI issues mean I can't handle it.

My meals are shakes and cream of wheat and ice cream. Nothing that needs teeth. Nothing with texture. Nothing that gives my jaw a job.

So I don't get to chew.

And chewing isn't just about food. It's a kind of input. It's rhythmic. It's grounding. It gives my mouth and my body something to do. When the food stopped needing it, the need didn't go away. It just looked for somewhere else to live.

It found my fingers.

## The Bigger Quiet

The food is only part of it.

I don't get much stimulation here at all. The same room. The same routine. The same textures and sounds and people, day after day. For a brain that runs on input, that's draining.

Nobody means for it to be that way. A nursing home is built to keep people safe and fed and clean. It isn't built to keep a thirty-four-year-old's mind fed. The quiet is just what the place is.

But the quiet has a cost. When the environment stops giving you input, your body goes looking for it. That's not a behavior problem. That's a body doing what bodies do.

## What the Chew Actually Is

For a long time I was told not to chew. Not to stim. That it would make me look a certain way.

That worry came from a version of my life that no longer matches reality. When everyone assumed I'd be mostly independent, a chew looked like a step backward. Like something that would draw the wrong kind of attention.

Now we know independence isn't the path. So the goal shifts. It stops being "look as typical as possible" and becomes "live as well as possible."

A chew that keeps me from chewing my fingers is just a good tool. It's me noticing a gap and filling it myself.

I'm an adult. It's my body and my stim. I don't need anyone's permission anymore.

## The Quiet Is the Problem

The chew tool isn't the story. The story is what it's standing in for.

I'm not getting food that needs chewing. I'm not getting much stimulation at all. And my body, left with a quiet it wasn't built for, found a way to make its own input.

The chew is in my mouth a lot now. It's my body telling me what it needs, and me finally listening instead of fighting it.
