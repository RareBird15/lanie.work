I tried to set up a GoFundMe the way you're supposed to. I'd be the organizer and the beneficiary. The money would go into my ABLE account, which is the account the federal government created specifically so disabled people could receive funds without losing benefits. That's the whole point of an ABLE account. It exists to solve this exact problem.

GoFundMe wouldn't let me do it.

The issue is that ABLE accounts are classified as investment accounts. Most ABLE plans, including mine through Massachusetts, are structured that way. They have a cash component, but at the account level, they're investment accounts. And GoFundMe, like most fundraising platforms, requires a checking or savings account to deposit funds. Investment accounts don't qualify.

I checked other platforms. Same problem. Most of them want a traditional bank account. An ABLE account, despite being designed for exactly this purpose, doesn't count.

## What ABLE Accounts Were Supposed to Fix

The ABLE Act passed in 2014. It created a new type of tax-advantaged savings account for people whose disabilities began before age 26. The idea was simple: disabled people should be able to save and receive money without risking their Medicaid, SSI, or other benefits.

Before ABLE, the rules were brutal. If you're on SSI, you can't have more than $2,000 in assets. That's not a typo. Two thousand dollars. If you go over that number, you lose your benefits. Your medical coverage, your monthly income, everything.

ABLE accounts were supposed to change that. Money in an ABLE account doesn't count toward that $2,000 limit. You can save. You can receive gifts. You can hold funds for disability-related expenses without the government treating you like you've suddenly become self-sufficient because someone gave you $500.

This is the account the government built for disabled people to receive money. And the fundraising platforms won't deposit to it.

## The Workaround

I made my mom the beneficiary of my GoFundMe. The money goes to her checking account, not my ABLE account. She manages it for me, and it stays out of my name for benefits purposes.

It works. But it shouldn't have to work this way.

I'm an adult. I should be able to receive money raised on my behalf in an account designed for exactly that purpose. Instead, I'm routing funds through my mother because a fundraising platform's payment processor can't tell the difference between an investment account and an ABLE account.

## The One Exception That Proves the Rule

I thought there might be one platform that works. Help Hope Live is a nonprofit medical fundraising organization. They cover transplants, catastrophic injuries, and catastrophic illnesses. They emphasize keeping your Medicaid, which means they understand the benefits landscape better than most. They hold funds as a 501(c)(3) and pay medical vendors directly instead of giving you cash, so the money never counts as your personal asset. That's smart. It solves the benefits problem in a different way.

But they can't deposit to ABLE accounts either. They can't accept transfers from ABLE accounts, and they can't transfer their fundraising balances into one. The two systems don't talk to each other.

And Help Hope Live is specifically for medical needs. People use it to raise money for cancer treatments, power chairs, surgeries. Big, clear medical expenses.

Some of what I need is medical. A lot of it isn't. Accessible technology isn't a medical expense. A computer that lets me write, communicate, and work isn't a medical expense. Replacing a broken Braille display isn't a medical expense. The line between "medical" and "disability-related" is one the system draws, not one I experience.

My fundraiser covers medical costs, accessible technology, and living costs. That's the reality of being disabled. The needs don't come in categories. They come all at once, and they overlap, and the platforms that serve one category won't serve the others.

So the one platform that understands the benefits landscape well enough to protect it still can't work with the account the government built for me. And the platforms whose rules my fundraiser does fit won't take my ABLE account.

## The Technical Excuse

I understand the technical explanation. Platforms use payment processors like Stripe and PayPal. Those processors verify account types before enabling deposits. When they see "investment account," they reject it, because most investment accounts aren't set up to receive third-party payments the way a checking account is.

But that's a technical limitation, not a policy one. ABLE accounts are federally recognized. They have unique tax rules. They're explicitly designed for disability-related funds. A platform that claims to help people raise money for medical and accessibility needs should be able to figure out how to deposit into the account the government created for that purpose.

The fact that nobody has figured this out yet tells me something. It tells me that the people building these platforms haven't talked to enough disabled people who actually rely on ABLE accounts. It tells me that "accessibility" on these platforms means screen reader compatibility and alt text, not structural accessibility in how money moves.

## The Bigger Picture

This isn't just about GoFundMe. It's about every platform that wants disabled people to use their services for fundraising while making it impossible to use the financial tools designed for us.

Disabled people are the ones who need fundraising the most. We're more likely to be low-income. We're more likely to have medical costs insurance won't cover. We're more likely to need community support to bridge gaps that benefits programs were never designed to fill.

And the accounts we're given to manage that support without losing our benefits? The platforms won't touch them.

The ABLE Act was a victory. It was hard-won disability policy that solved a real problem. But policy victories don't mean much if the infrastructure doesn't follow. An account nobody can deposit into isn't an account. It's a line item on a government brochure.

I have an ABLE account with a balance of $43.30. I have a GoFundMe that's raised $1,466. The GoFundMe money can't go into the ABLE account. So it goes to my mom instead.

That's not how this is supposed to work.
